    Section 1.2 Conditionals and Biconditionals

    Subsection 1.2.1 Conditionals

    Conditionals are statements that are logical inferences: statements of the form “If \(p\text{,}\) then \(q\)”. We write such inferences as \(p \implies q\) and read it as “\(p\) implies \(q\text{.}\)”
    🔗
    Note: Though this language (“\(p\) implies \(q\)”) is very common, it is often misleading. This language might make you think that \(q\) can be, in some sense, deduced from \(p\text{,}\) and that the truth of \(p\) is necessary for \(p \implies q\) to be meaningful. This is not the case, as we will soon see.
    🔗
    For example:
    🔗

    Example 1.2.1.

    If \(a\) and \(b\) are the lengths of the legs of a right triangle with hypotenuse \(c\text{,}\) then \(a^2 + b^2 = c^2\)
    🔗
    🔗
    Or:
    🔗

    Example 1.2.2.

    If 288 is divisible by 9, then 288 is divisible by 3.
    🔗
    🔗
    The truth of \(p\) gives us information to update our knowledge about the truth of \(q\text{;}\) that is, the truth of \(q\) is conditioned on \(p\text{.}\)
    🔗

    Definition 1.2.3.

    Let \(p\) and \(q\) be propositions. The conditional statement \(p \implies q\) is the proposition “if \(p\text{,}\) then \(q\text{.}\)” The conditional statement \(p \implies q\) is false when \(p\) is true and \(q\) is false, and true otherwise. In the conditional statement \(p \implies q\text{,}\) \(p\) is called the hypothesis (or antecedent or premise) and \(q\) is called the conclusion (or consequence).
    🔗
    🔗
    As a truth table:
    🔗
    \(p\) \(q\) \(p \implies q\)
    \(T\) \(T\) \(T\)
    \(T\) \(F\) \(F\)
    \(F\) \(T\) \(T\)
    \(F\) \(F\) \(T\)
    The first two rows are likely expected, based on the discussion above. The bottom two rows might be confusing, but they are consistent with the idea that the truth of \(q\) is conditioned on the truth of \(p\text{.}\)
    🔗
    A conditional statement that is true by virtue of the fact that its hypothesis is false (i.e., the bottom two rows) is called vacuously true, because the statement itself is true, it just does not apply in this particular situation. For example, consider our example from above:
    🔗

    Example 1.2.4.

    If \(a\) and \(b\) are the lengths of the legs of a right triangle with hypotenuse \(c\text{,}\) then \(a^2 + b^2 = c^2\)
    🔗
    🔗
    If \((a, b, c)\) is not a Pythagorean triple (i.e., \(p\) is false), the implication is still true, it’s just not relevant.
    🔗

    Subsubsection 1.2.1.1 Representation as an “Or” Statement

    I claim: \(p \implies q \equiv q \lor \lnot p\text{.}\)
    🔗

    Example 1.2.5.

    Proof:
    🔗
    \(p\) \(q\) \(p \implies q\) \(\lnot p\) \(q \lor \lnot p\)
    \(T\) \(T\) \(T\) \(F\) \(T\)
    \(T\) \(F\) \(F\) \(F\) \(F\)
    \(F\) \(T\) \(T\) \(T\) \(T\)
    \(F\) \(F\) \(T\) \(T\) \(T\)
    🔗
    This is more profound than it seems. This implies that logical inferences can be reduced to simpler logical connectives. This is how computers implement more sophisticated logical operations. Computers cannot natively handle implications, but they can handle AND, OR, and NOT (using voltage signals and transistors).
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsubsection 1.2.1.2 Contrapositive, Converse, and Inverse

    There are three different types of statements that are related to implications:
    🔗

    Contrapositive.

    Definition 1.2.6.
    The contrapositive of the statement “If \(p\text{,}\) then \(q\)” (i.e., \(p \implies q\)) is the statement “If not \(q\text{,}\) then not \(p\)” (i.e., \(\lnot q \implies \lnot p\)).
    🔗
    🔗
    The following fact is very important, and is often used in mathematical proofs:
    🔗
    Theorem 1.2.7.
    The contrapositive of an implication is logically equivalent to the original implication. That is, \(p \implies q \equiv \lnot q \implies \lnot p\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    Proof.
    Homework.
    🔗
    🔗
    Why is this so important? Because it can often simplify difficult problems into much simpler ones.
    🔗
    Example 1.2.8.
    What is the converse of “If \(n^2\) is even, then \(n\) is even”? It is “If \(n\) is odd, then \(n^2\) is odd.” This is much easier to prove!
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Converse.

    Definition 1.2.9.
    The converse of the statement “If \(p\text{,}\) then \(q\)” (i.e., \(p \implies q\)) is the statement “If \(q\text{,}\) then \(p\)” (i.e., \(q \implies p\)).
    🔗
    🔗
    Is a conditional logically equivalent to its converse? No! (Prove this.)
    🔗
    🔗

    Inverse.

    Definition 1.2.10.
    The inverse of the statement “If \(p\text{,}\) then \(q\)” (i.e., \(p \implies q\)) is the statement “If not \(p\text{,}\) then not \(q\)” (i.e., \(\lnot p \implies \lnot q\)).
    🔗
    🔗
    Is a conditional logically equivalent to its inverse? No! (Prove this.)
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection 1.2.2 Biconditionals

    Definition 1.2.11.

    Let \(p\) and \(q\) be propositions. The biconditional statement \(p \iff q\) is the proposition “\(p\) if and only if \(q\text{.}\)” The biconditional statement \(p \iff q\) is true when \(p\) and \(q\) have the same truth values, and false otherwise.
    🔗
    🔗
    \(p\) \(q\) \(p \iff q\)
    \(T\) \(T\) \(T\)
    \(T\) \(F\) \(F\)
    \(F\) \(T\) \(F\)
    \(F\) \(F\) \(T\)
    Claim: \(p \iff q \equiv (p \implies q) \land (q \implies p)\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Note: Since \(p \implies q\) can be written in terms of AND, OR, and NOT, \(p \iff q\) can also be written in terms of AND, OR, and NOT.
    🔗

    Subsubsection 1.2.2.1 Necessary and Sufficient Conditions

    If \(p\) and \(q\) are statements:
    🔗
    • \(p\) is a sufficient condition for \(q\) means “if \(p\) then \(q\text{.}\)”
      🔗
    • \(p\) is a necessary condition for \(q\) means “if not \(p\) then not \(q\text{.}\)”
      🔗
    🔗
    Using the contrapositive, we see that \(p\) is a necessary condition for \(q\) equivalently means “if \(q\) then \(p\text{.}\)” Hence, \(p \iff q\) means that \(p\) is a necessary and sufficient condition for \(q\) (and equivalently \(q\) is a necessary and sufficient condition for \(p\)).
    🔗
