Theorem 5.5.1.

Let \(a \in \mathbb{Z}\) and \(m \in \mathbb{N}\text{.}\) Then:
🔗
  1. There exists some \(b \in \mathbb{Z}\) such that \(ab \equiv 1\) (mod \(m\)) if and only if \(\mathrm{gcd}(a, m) = 1\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
  2. If \(ab_1 \equiv ab_2 \equiv 1\) (mod \(m\)), then \(b_1 \equiv b_2\) (mod \(m\)).
    🔗
    🔗
🔗
If such a number \(b\) exists, then we write \(b = a^{-1}\) and call it the inverse of \(a\) modulo \(m\text{.}\)
🔗
🔗

Proof.

  1. \((\impliedby)\) Suppose \(\mathrm{gcd}(a, m) = 1\text{.}\) Then, by Corollary 5.3.7, there exists integers \(x\) and \(y\) such that \(ax + my = 1\text{.}\) Therefore, \(ax = 1 - my \equiv 1\) (mod \(m\)). Letting \(b = x\) completes the proof.
    🔗
    \((\implies)\) Assume \(ab \equiv 1\) (mod \(m\)). Then there exists an integer \(k\) such that \(ab = 1 - km\text{,}\) which implies \(ab + km = 1\text{.}\) We know from the proof of Theorem 5.3.6 that \(\mathrm{gcd}(a, m) \mid (ab + km)\) and therefore \(\mathrm{gcd}(a, m) \mid 1\text{.}\) Hence, \(\mathrm{gcd}(a, m) = 1\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
  2. Notice:
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} b_1 \equiv b_1 \cdot 1 \equiv b_1 \cdot (ab_2) \equiv (ab_1) \cdot b_2 \equiv 1 \cdot b_2 \equiv b_2. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Hence, \(b_1 \equiv b_2\) (mod \(m\)).
    🔗
    🔗
🔗
